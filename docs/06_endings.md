# The Endings

Four endings, read by the Architect over painted watercolour vignettes. Selection order is in the design bible §9. Each epilogue references people by name; lines in [brackets] are conditional on the player's history and are listed after each ending.

---

## The Diplomat

*High Integrity. Every room a clean or partial win.*

There is a Monday after every room. This is an account of some of them.

Hemant Joshi still walks Parvati at a quarter past six. A hundred and three steps to the first landing. In the second year of your loan the shop's overdraft cleared, because a coaching centre opened next door and every student in it needed forty pages photocopied by morning. He did not tell you. You heard from your mother, who heard from his wife, who said he had started wearing the good shirt on ordinary days.

Gillian Pryce was graded up in June. She retired three years later and took the date stamp home, which is against policy, and keeps it on the windowsill above her sink. Some mornings she sets it to the day and stamps the back of her hand.

Your father files your returns in two countries. He is wrong about one Schedule FA rule every year, and when you point it out he says the rule has changed, and sometimes it has. The photograph of his own father is face-up on the sideboard. He has not mentioned it. You have not asked.

Nabil Haddad's daughters are teenagers now and no longer make bread with him on Sundays. He makes it alone. It comes out properly most weeks. [If Room 4 clean:] He sent you a photograph of one once, without a message, from a number you had forgotten you had.

Olivia Marsh left the company the year after you did. Her mother is in remission. She swims in the ladies' pond in January and posts about it, and you like the posts, and you have not spoken since.

Tom Whitcombe understands the Bayesian prior now. He explained it at a conference in Leeds. He thanked "a colleague" from the stage. He meant you, and he said so in an email afterwards, three lines long, with a photograph of a chilli plant.

Kwame Asante-Boateng renewed twice. Then a larger freight company bought his business, and the new owners didn't need your system, and that was that. He sent a Hearts of Oak scarf the first Christmas and nothing after. The orchid, Efua says, is still alive.

Dev Malhotra followed on at your Series A. At your Series B he didn't, and he told you why, to your face, in the glass room: your growth was real and his fund's mandate had changed. You believed him. You were right to. His daughter works three streets from your office. You have never met her.

Richard Haldane retired. The puffins, according to the station's newsletter, which you found yourself reading, were late again.

Fiona Blake works at a charity in Hackney. In March she backed a complaint against a trustee, and lost, and stayed.

Imran Shah's juniper is on his desk. It is over-pruned. He waters it on Thursdays, after his appointment. He does not mention the stairwell, and neither do you, and when he passes your desk he knocks twice on it with one knuckle, which is a thing he started doing and has not explained.

In the War Room you did not find a culprit, because there wasn't one. The merger collapsed. Meera Rao kept her firm, smaller, and her practising certificate. Daniel Fernandes made partner the following year. Priya Nair's new sponsor is a firm in Canary Wharf, and her mother tells everyone in Thrissur that Ponnu is very senior now. Karan Sethi went back to the Bartlett. He climbs on Wednesdays. He carries his father's reading glasses in his jacket and has never needed them. Fatima Sheikh's report is on the regulator's website. Your name is not in it, because you didn't do anything that belonged in a report. You brought her three true things.

None of this was a triumph.

You lost things. The seed round at a lower valuation than you could have got with a bluff. A year of your father's silence that a flattering lie would have shortened. Kwame, in the end, to a company that didn't need you. The job in Room 8, which you kept for six months and then left, because winning cleanly in that room meant being someone they would never promote.

You kept your hands clean, and clean hands are still hands. They still held what they could and dropped the rest.

On a Tuesday in October you were asked, in a meeting, whether you'd sign something today. You said you'd read it first. Nobody in the room noticed anything. It was an ordinary sentence. It took you nine rooms to be able to say it like that.

The Architect has nothing to add.

If the Imran corridor was a partial, his paragraph reads: "Imran Shah is okay. He called someone two days later than he said he would. You think about the two days."]*

## The Operator

*Low Integrity. Five or more rooms won.*

You won.

This is an inventory.

The Kitchen in Kasba Peth is a kitchen. There is a pressure cooker on the burner and a steel tumbler on the shelf. Hemant Joshi still lives two floors up from where he was born. He is not in the kitchen. He has not been in a room with you since the wedding where your mother sat you at different tables and pretended it was the seating plan.

The Records desk in Manchester has a new officer. Gillian Pryce retired early. The incident report is in a file. The file is in a basement. Nobody will ever read it, and it is there.

The dining table in Pune has the plastic tablecloth with mangoes on it. Three thalis. One is not set. Your father has a junior now, Joglekar's son, whose name is on the board outside the office in fresh paint. When you call, your mother answers. She passes the phone to your father. He says "Haan," and "Accha," and "Your mother wants to talk," and gives the phone back.

The office above the phone shop in Rusholme is let to a cousin of a cousin. Nabil sold his half of the building to Karim. The brothers speak at Eid.

Kew, the meeting room in Farringdon, has a new whiteboard. The new agenda doesn't have your initials on it, or Tom's.

The client's office in Park Royal is someone else's office. The orchid died.

The glass room on the thirty-first floor still has a view. Dev Malhotra sits in it with founders who did not bluff him. He describes you, when asked, as "green," which is the kindest word he has.

The boardroom has a long table and one man at the head of it, and it isn't Richard any more, and it doesn't matter, because the NDA is forty pages and does not expire.

The War Room is empty. The map was taken down by the police.

These are the rooms. You are not in any of them.

Here is what you are owed. The Architect keeps accounts.

You called nineteen tactics, in nine rooms, by their proper names. Each was a person.

Hemant Joshi. Hemant Joshi. Gillian Pryce. Ramesh Deshpande. Nabil Haddad. Nabil Haddad. Olivia Marsh. Tom Whitcombe. Tom Whitcombe. Kwame Asante-Boateng. Dev Malhotra. Dev Malhotra. Richard Haldane. Fiona Blake. Fiona Blake. Meera Rao. Daniel Fernandes. Daniel Fernandes.

And the last. The one in the grey room. It didn't have a name. It had yours.

You know what every one of them was doing to you. You knew it in the moment. You named it, and then, in most of those rooms, you did it back, better, and it worked.

It worked. This is not a story in which it didn't. You have the money from the Board and the seed round at the number you wanted, and Nabil's £950, and the transcript on time, and your name on slide six. You have a flat with a view of the river and a job title with two words in it that most people never get.

You have a phone with a lot of numbers in it that do not call.

On a Tuesday in October you were asked, in a meeting, whether you'd sign something today. You read the room. You saw the deadline was manufactured, and the flattery was flattery, and the man to the left of the chair was the good cop. You named every one of them to yourself, and you were right about all of it, and you signed, because you'd worked out it was in your interest, and it was.

When you got home there was no one to tell.

The Architect closes the book.

## The Wall

*The clean no in Room 9.*

A door.

It is an ordinary door, painted white a long time ago, with a brass handle that has been turned by a lot of hands. You open it.

A corridor. Carpet tiles, grey, one of them a slightly different grey where it was replaced. A fire extinguisher. A noticeboard with a poster about a five-a-side league and a lost-and-found notice for a blue umbrella. Someone has written "found it, thanks" on the notice in biro and nobody has taken it down.

The lift is out of order. There is a sign.

You take the stairs.

Six floors. On the third-floor landing there is a window, and through it, the street. It is early. A man is putting out the chairs in front of a café. He puts out four and stands back and moves one.

Ground floor. A security desk. The guard is reading a laminated card. He looks up and nods, and you nod.

Outside.

It's Monday.

It has rained in the night. The pavement is darker in the cracks. A bus goes past, half empty, the number on the front changing from one destination to another as it pulls away.

You stand there for a while.

A delivery van double-parks. The driver gets out with a box under each arm and goes into the building next door, and leaves the engine running, and nobody minds.

A pigeon walks along the kerb, looks at you, and decides you are not interesting.

Somebody's radio, from an upstairs window. The traffic report. A lane closed on the A40. Delays of about ten minutes. Then a song you half know, and the window closes, and the song is gone.

Nobody asks you anything.

Nobody needs anything from you by noon.

In your bag there is a document. You haven't read it yet. You will read it on the train, all of it, slowly, including the clauses at the end in the smaller type. If you agree with it, you'll sign it. If you don't, you won't. Either way you'll decide.

There's a man at the crossing with a dog. The dog sits when the light is red, without being told.

A woman two doors down is pulling up the shutter of a shop. It sticks halfway, and she reaches up and hits it, once, with the flat of her hand, and it goes up the rest of the way.

Your phone buzzes. You look at it. It's your father. A link to an HMRC page, and three typos, and "call when free."

You put the phone away. You'll call when you're free.

The café man has finished with the chairs. He goes inside. The door swings shut behind him and then, because the latch is loose, swings a little way open again, and stays there, open about the width of a hand.

You start walking.

The light changes.

It's a Monday. You have a document in your bag that you haven't signed. The day is ahead of you. It is not remarkable. Nobody will remember it but you.

You walk past the café. Through the open door you can smell coffee, and something with cardamom in it, and you think about going in, and you don't, and you think you might tomorrow.

A boy in a school blazer runs past, late, his bag bouncing, a slice of toast in one hand. He doesn't look at anyone. He has somewhere to be, and he will get there, slightly out of breath, and it will be fine.

At the end of the street you stop at the crossing. The man with the dog has gone. The light is red.

You wait.

It turns green.

You'll know, by Waterloo, what you agreed to." The final two lines are unchanged.]*

## The Scapegoat

*The War Room dark win.*

Daniel Fernandes was interviewed under caution at Holborn police station from 2:10 in the afternoon until 11:05 at night. He asked for water four times and was brought it three. His solicitor, a woman named Hamilton whom he had trained with, told him twice to say nothing, and he said nothing for the first six hours, and then he told them about the shouting, and the partnership, and the time on the visitor log, and how he had lied about it because it looked terrible. It did look terrible. He was released on bail at 11:20. A photographer was outside. The next morning his face was on page seven of two papers and the front of one, cropped from a firm brochure, smiling. His mother in Wembley bought all three. She put them in a drawer in the kitchen, under the tea towels, where she keeps things she has not decided what to do with.

On Sunday at four o'clock, Priya Nair's phone rang in a cousin's flat in East Ham, where she had been staying since Thursday, and it was her mother in Thrissur, as it is every Sunday at four. Priya was sitting in the kitchen with her coat on. Her mother asked if she had eaten. Priya said yes. Her mother asked about work. Priya said work was difficult just now. Her mother said, "Ponnu, what's happened, your voice is different," and Priya said, "Amma, I can't explain, I'll explain, I'll explain later," and her mother waited, and Priya didn't. On the table in front of her was a letter from the firm, received Friday, about her sponsorship being "under review pending the outcome of the inquiry." She had read it eleven times. The Krishna mask was still on her desk in Holborn. She had not been allowed back to collect it.

Karan Sethi read every article about Daniel Fernandes for three weeks. He read them on the bus, and at the climbing wall between routes, and in bed at two in the morning with the brightness turned down so his mother wouldn't see the light under the door. He read the comments under them. He read a thread on a forum for law students where someone who claimed to know Daniel said he'd always had a temper. He believed it. Then, in the fourth week, the articles stopped. Not a correction, not a retraction. They stopped. He searched Daniel's name every morning for a month and found nothing new, and nobody from the firm called, and nobody from the police called, and when he asked his mother she said the solicitor said these things take time. He kept his father's reading glasses in his shirt pocket. He began to wear shirts with pockets.

Daniel was released without charge on the twenty-third day. A two-line statement was issued by his solicitor. It was not reported.

The insurer froze the payout pending the inquiry. Sunita Sethi sold the house in Hampstead Garden Suburb the following spring.

Ishani, the daughter at university in Edinburgh who had not been told on the first morning, was told on the second. She came down for the funeral and went back up the day after. She has not been home since. This is not mentioned to anyone at the firm, because no one at the firm asks.

The Hallett Crane merger closed on the fourteenth, as scheduled. The press release described Arvind Sethi as "a much-loved colleague whose loss is deeply felt." Meera Rao's name was at the bottom of it.

Fatima Sheikh's report on the Estuary matter was filed with the police in the week Daniel was released. It ran to ninety-one pages. It did not mention you. She cycled from London to Birmingham in the rain that June, on the A5, alone.

Six months after the morning in the War Room, on a Thursday, Meera Rao sat in a small meeting room at a police station in Westminster with her solicitor and a detective sergeant. On the table was a statement, typed, eleven pages. She read every page. She asked for one word on page four to be changed. It was changed. She took a pen from her own bag, not the one on the table, and signed at the bottom of each page, and dated it, and initialled the change on page four. The detective sergeant thanked her. She put the pen back in her bag.

You never see the statement.

You were cleared. Meera gave you her word, and she kept it.

You still have your job. Your manager is pleasant to you. Your calendar is full.

The courier never came. At noon on that first morning, the clock above the War Room door said 11:56. It was four minutes slow. Arvind always said he'd fix it.

Nobody has.
